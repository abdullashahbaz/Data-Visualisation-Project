Does Money Win Medals?

The intuition is straightforward: wealthier nations can fund better training facilities, coaching staff, sports science programmes, and athlete development pathways. But how strong is this relationship in practice?

Each bubble represents a nation. Its horizontal position reflects average GDP per capita (log scale); its vertical position shows total medals won. Bubble size encodes the number of athletes sent. The map shows medal efficiency - medals per $10 000 of GDP/capita - revealing nations that punch above their economic weight.

What the Data Tells Us

Across 56 years of Summer Olympics, three findings stand out. Wealth matters - but it is neither sufficient nor strictly necessary. Sporting culture, political investment, and historical contingency all leave clear signatures in the data.

Chapter 2

Who Has Dominated the Olympics?

The charts above show wealth and efficiency in aggregate. This bump chart shows how the medal-table rank of the top 15 nations changed across every Summer Olympics from 1960 to 2016. Rank 1 means most medals that year - the best position is at the top of the chart.

Two dashed vertical lines mark the 1980 Moscow boycott (USA absent) and the 1984 Los Angeles boycott (USSR absent) - moments where geopolitics visibly warped the medal table. Notice how lines disappear and reappear around those years. Click any line or country label to select that nation.

Key insight: The USA and Soviet Union traded top-two positions throughout the Cold War era. After Soviet dissolution in 1991, Russia's rank dropped sharply as medals fragmented across newly independent states. China's dramatic rise from the mid-1990s permanently reshaped the top of the table, reaching rank 1 at the 2008 Beijing home Games.
